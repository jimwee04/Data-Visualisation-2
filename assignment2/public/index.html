Food Availability

How Australia's food supply has changed since 2010, from paddock to port to the supermarket trolley

Research question

Australia grows far more food than it uses. So which foods are truly plentiful, which do we rely on the world for, and how has what reaches our plates changed since 2010?

Grow1 of 7

Where our food grows

Most Australians never see a farm. The food in an average trolley comes from very different landscapes, and some of it comes almost entirely from a single state.

What it means. Victoria and NSW produce the most food in total, but they also have the most mouths to feed. Per resident, Tasmania ($3,842) and WA ($3,743) lead, and NSW ranks last of the states. Some foods hang on one region: Victoria produces 61% of the nation's milk value, Queensland 95% of its sugarcane, and WA and NSW split two-thirds of the grain.

Your fruit bowl and your milk carton come from a handful of places.

Stock2 of 7

A surplus country with an uneven pantry

Whether Australia "has enough food" depends on which food you mean. For the staples we grow far more than we use. For a few everyday groups we rely on the rest of the world.

What it means. Cereals, pulses, sugar and meat are in deep surplus, and most of it is shipped overseas. Seafood is the clear exception, with two-thirds of supply imported. Dairy is the staple losing its buffer: milk production peaked at 10.0 Mt in 2016 and fell to 8.5 Mt in 2023 while imports rose 70%, pushing it from 118 to just 103 kg grown per 100 kg used.

We grow 3½ times the grain we use, but only about 1 kg in every 3 kg of seafood we use is caught or farmed here.

Ship3 of 7

Where the surplus goes

The surplus doesn't stay here. Australia shipped US$38.5 billion of food overseas in 2024 and brought in US$17.1 billion, and the two flows look very different.

What it means. Exports are bulk commodities headed to Asia and North America: beef to China, the US, Japan and Korea, wheat to Indonesia and South-East Asia, and lentils to India. Imports are mostly processed foods, and Europe as a whole is the largest source (31%): wine from France, olive oil from Spain and pork from Denmark and the Netherlands, alongside food preparations from New Zealand and pork from the US.

Our wheat feeds Indonesia; our lentils feed India; our pork comes from America.

Plate4 of 7

What changed on our plates

What's available shapes what we eat. Since 2010 the mix of food available to each Australian has shifted, and the biggest move has been in the meat aisle.

What it means. Beef supply per person fell from 38.6 kg to 26.9 kg (−30%) as about 69% of beef production went to export, while chicken rose from 46.5 kg to 53.1 kg. Fruit supply dropped from 102 kg to a 74 kg low in 2019 and has only partly recovered.

Australians now have twice as much chicken as beef available, and less fruit, than in 2010.

Shock5 of 7

Shocks in the shopping trolley

Availability isn't only a long-term story. Supermarket sales react within weeks to seasons, holidays and crises.

What it means. In March 2020 total food sales jumped 18% on February, with bread, flour and pasta up 45% and vegetables up 24%, then fell below normal in April as households ate through their stockpiles. Each year has its rhythm too: December is the peak month in five of six years, and fruit buying dips every June–July.

In March 2020 we bought 45% more flour and pasta, then stopped.

Checkout6 of 7

Available is not the same as bought

Two independent sources measure the plate from different ends: FAO estimates what is available, the ABS counts what is bought at the supermarket. Where they agree, the trend is real; where they split, something happens between the farm gate and the checkout.

What it means. Chicken rises in both sources, so that shift is real. Fruit and dairy milk split: supply recovered after 2019 but supermarket buying fell (fruit −8%, dairy milk −7%), while plant-based milk sales grew 33%. Fruit is becoming more valuable (farm-gate value +25% since 2020-21) rather than more bought; price is a likely link but is not tested here.

Verdict7 of 7

So, how available is our food?

  • Surplus staples. Australia grows 3½ times its grain, 3 times its pulses and 1.6 times its meat. Droughts shrink exports, not the bread aisle.
  • Thin margins. Two-thirds of our seafood is imported, and dairy has slipped to just above "only enough", with most of it produced in one state.
  • Changing plates. Chicken has replaced beef, fruit buying is falling, and sudden shocks like March 2020 show up in the trolley within weeks.

Next time you shop, check the label: notice what's grown here, and what had to travel to reach you.
