Where our food grows

Most Australians never see a farm. The food in a typical trolley comes from very different landscapes, and some of it from just one state.

Key takeaway

Several food groups are concentrated in one state, so one bad season in Victoria would hit most of our milk.

Stock2 of 7

A surplus country with an uneven pantry

Having enough food depends on which food. Australia grows far more staples than it needs, yet relies on imports for some everyday foods.

Key takeaway

Grain, pulses, sugar and meat are truly plentiful. We rely on the world for seafood, and dairy nearly does too.

Ship3 of 7

A food’s travel diary

The surplus does not stay here. In 2024, Australia exported US$38.5 billion of food and imported US$17.1 billion. Seafood is not in these flows, because FAO trade data covers farm products only.

Key takeaway

For staples we supply the world, while we rely on others mainly for processed food.

Plate4 of 7

How our plates changed

What is available shapes what we eat. Since 2010, the food available to each Australian has shifted, most of all in the meat aisle.

Key takeaway

Chicken replaced beef. Fruit supply has partly recovered from its 2019 low but is still 16% below 2010.

Shock5 of 7

Flash changes in the trolley

Availability is not only a long-term story. Supermarket sales react within weeks to seasons, holidays and crises.

Key takeaway

The panic spike passed within a month, but vegetables, meat and milk stayed higher for the rest of 2020.

Checkout6 of 7

Available is not the same as bought

Two sources measure the plate from different ends. FAO estimates what is available; the ABS counts what is bought at the supermarket.

Key takeaway

Supply is not the limit. While more fruit and milk are available, we buy less.

Verdict7 of 7

So, how available is our food?

Australia has more than enough food overall. But the surplus sits in a few staple foods such as grain, some everyday foods such as seafood and processed foods depend on imports, and our plates have changed through exports and choices, not shortages.

  • Which foods are truly plentiful?

    Grain, pulses, sugar and meat. We grow 1.6 to 3.5 times what we use, so droughts cut exports, not supply.

  • Which do we rely on the world for?

    Seafood and processed food. We catch or farm only 37 kg of every 100 kg of seafood we use, and processed foods lead our imports. Dairy is close behind: its surplus has shrunk to 3%.

  • How has what reaches our plates changed?

    Chicken has replaced beef, as most beef is exported. Fruit supply has partly recovered from its 2019 low but is still 16% below 2010, and we buy less. The 2020 panic spike passed within a month.

  • Next time you shop, check the label and notice what was grown here.

Notes

  • Supply (FAO) includes food later wasted; sales (ABS) exclude cafés and takeaway. Compare trends, not levels.
  • FAO values are model estimates; 2021–23 seafood figures are carried forward.
  • The two state maps count grains, pulses, oilseeds, sugarcane, fruit, vegetables, nuts, milk and livestock sold, but not wool or wine grapes. The Australian Capital Territory ($24 per resident) is labelled offshore. Map colour classes are round steps around the national average of A$2,680 per resident.
  • The spike map places each spike at a representative point inside its state, not at the farms; states under 3% are unlabelled.
  • The self-sufficiency and waffle charts leave out vegetables, because FAO counts processed imports such as tomato paste at fresh weight. "Used here" covers food eaten by people and fed to livestock. The waffle chart also leaves out eggs, since 95% are grown and used here. The waffle chart shows FAO’s stock change as “added to stocks” (29% of 2023 sugar). The dairy chart counts dairy products as their liquid-milk equivalent; its step order is a choice, so only its totals are real years.
  • The trade map and export flow diagram cover food only (no wool, cotton, hides, tobacco or animal feed) and do not include fish or seafood. Europe's 38 trading countries share one corridor, so they are one flow. The flow diagram groups countries into regions, while the trade map shows single countries. In the flow diagram, "other food" includes sorghum, almonds, sugar, live cattle and processed foods.
  • The available-versus-bought chart compares FAO calendar years 2019 to 2023 with ABS financial years 2018-19 to 2022-23. 2019 is the first year both cover, and fruit supply’s lowest year.
  • The heatmap compares each month with its financial year’s average, leaving March 2020 out of that average; the box plot compares each month with the month before.
  • Production values are A$ farm-gate; trade values are US$. They are never added together.
  • ABS consumption data are labelled "experimental".
