Grow1 of 7

Where our food grows

Most Australians never see a farm. The food in a typical trolley comes from very different landscapes, and some of it from just one state.

What it means. Victoria and New South Wales grow the most food, but they also feed the most people. Per resident, Tasmania and Western Australia lead. Some foods rely on one state: Victoria produces 61% of our milk value and Queensland 95% of our sugarcane.

Key takeawayOur food is concentrated, so one bad season in Victoria would hit most of our milk.

Stock2 of 7

A surplus country with an uneven pantry

Having enough food depends on which food. Australia grows far more staples than it needs, yet relies on imports for some everyday foods.

What it means. Cereals, pulses, sugar and meat are in deep surplus, and much of the extra is exported. Seafood is the exception: two-thirds of it is imported. Dairy is losing its buffer, falling from 118 kg to 103 kg grown for every 100 kg used. Even in the 2017–19 drought, cereal supply per person held steady.

Key takeawayGrain, pulses, sugar and meat are truly plentiful; seafood relies on the world, and dairy nearly does.

Ship3 of 7

Where the surplus goes

The surplus does not stay here. In 2024, Australia exported US$38.5 billion of food and imported US$17.1 billion, and the two flows look very different.

What it means. We export bulk commodities to Asia and North America: beef to China, the United States and Japan, wheat to South-East Asia and lentils to India. We import mostly processed foods, such as wine, olive oil and pork. Europe is the largest source, at 31%.

Key takeawayFor staples we supply the world; we rely on others mainly for processed food.

Plate4 of 7

What changed on our plates

What is available shapes what we eat. Since 2010, the food available to each Australian has shifted, most of all in the meat aisle.

What it means. Beef supply fell from 38.6 kg to 26.9 kg per person, as about 69% of production was exported. Chicken rose to 53.1 kg. Fruit fell to a low of 74 kg in 2019 and has only partly recovered.

Key takeawayBeef gave way to chicken, but nothing replaced the fruit we lost.

Shock5 of 7

Shocks in the shopping trolley

Availability is not only a long-term story. Supermarket sales react within weeks to seasons, holidays and crises.

What it means. In March 2020, total food sales jumped 18%, led by bread, flour and pasta at +45%. In April, sales fell below normal as households ate their stockpiles. In most years, December is the busiest month, and fruit buying dips every June and July.

Key takeawayShocks pass quickly: even the 2020 panic was over within two months.

Checkout6 of 7

Available is not the same as bought

Two sources measure the plate from different ends. FAO estimates what is available; the ABS counts what is bought at the supermarket. Where they agree, the trend is real. Where they split, something happens between farm and checkout.

What it means. Chicken rises in both sources, so that shift is real. For fruit and dairy milk, supply recovered but buying fell, by 8% and 7%. Plant-based milk sales grew 33%. Fruit’s farm-gate value has risen 25% since 2020-21, so price is a likely cause, though not tested here.

Key takeawaySupply is not the limit: we buy less fruit and milk though more is available.

Verdict7 of 7

So, how available is our food?

Australia has more than enough food overall. But the surplus sits in a few staples, some everyday foods depend on imports, and our plates have changed through exports and choices, not shortages.

  • Which foods are truly plentiful?

    Grain, pulses, sugar and meat. We grow 1.6 to 3½ times what we use, so droughts cut exports, not supply.

  • Which do we rely on the world for?

    Seafood and processed food. Two-thirds of our seafood is imported, and processed foods make up most imports. Dairy is close behind: its surplus has shrunk to 3%.

  • How has what reaches our plates changed?

    Chicken has replaced beef, as most beef is exported. Fruit supply has recovered since 2019, yet we buy less, and shocks like March 2020 pass within weeks.

Next time you shop, check the label: notice what's grown here, and what had to travel to reach you.

About this visualisation

Data sources

  • FAOSTAT Food Balances (2010–2023), Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO)
  • FAOSTAT Detailed Trade Matrix (2022–2024), FAO
  • Apparent Consumption of Selected Foodstuffs 2023-24 (Tables 1.1 and 1.2), Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS)
  • Australian Agriculture 2024-25: Broadacre Crops, Horticulture, Livestock, ABS
  • National, state and territory population, ABS
  • ABS Australian Statistical Geography Standard 2021 state boundaries; Natural Earth / world-atlas

Notes

  • Supply (FAO) includes food later wasted; sales (ABS) exclude cafés and takeaway. Compare trends, not levels.
  • FAO values are model estimates; 2021–23 seafood figures are carried forward.
  • The two state maps count grains, pulses, oilseeds, sugarcane, fruit, vegetables, nuts, milk and livestock sold, but not wool or wine grapes. The Australian Capital Territory ($24 per resident) is too small to label.
  • The spike map places each spike at a representative point inside its state, not at the farms; states under 3% are unlabelled.
  • The self-sufficiency and waffle charts leave out vegetables, because FAO counts processed imports such as tomato paste at fresh weight. "Used here" covers food eaten by people and fed to livestock. The waffle chart also leaves out eggs, since 95% are grown and kept here. The dairy chart counts dairy products as their liquid-milk equivalent.
  • The trade map and export flow diagram cover food only (no wool, cotton, hides, tobacco or animal feed) and do not include fish or seafood. Europe's 38 trading countries share one corridor, so they are one flow. In the flow diagram, "other food" includes sorghum, almonds, sugar, live cattle and processed foods.
  • The available-versus-bought chart compares FAO calendar years 2019 to 2023 with ABS financial years 2018-19 to 2022-23.
  • Production values are A$ farm-gate; trade values are US$. They are never added together.
  • ABS consumption data are labelled "experimental".
